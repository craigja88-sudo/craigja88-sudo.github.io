Pricing

What dumpster rental costs, without the $99 bait

There is no honest nationwide “from $99” dumpster. A week in a typical US metro is usually hundreds of dollars, not ninety-nine. Below are wide ranges we see quoted for standard household debris — not a price guarantee.

SizeOften quoted (about 7 days)Notes
10 yard$250–$500Common for small remodels; heavy debris may still fill the weight cap first.
15 yard$300–$550Not offered by every hauler; some skip straight from 10 to 20.
20 yard$350–$700The volume most homeowners actually need.
30 yard$400–$800Contractor jobs; extra days add up.
40 yard$500–$1,000+Construction and swaps; ask about haul cycle, not just the first drop.

Tight, competitive markets (many Sun Belt metros) cluster toward the lower-middle of those bands. Higher-cost metros, long round-trips to the landfill, and peak storm or roofing season sit higher. Miami, Denver, and coastal cities can run above inland Texas on the same size — not because of a secret fee, because landfill and labor costs differ.

What changes the number

  • Weight allowance and overage. The quote includes a tonnage cap. Go over and you pay per extra ton, which is often the real bill on roofing and plaster.
  • Days. A “week” might be 7 calendar days or 5 business days. Extra days are cheaper than a second dumpster if you are close to done.
  • Distance. Delivery and dump-site mileage. Far suburbs and island-adjacent jobs cost more. Fuel surcharges appear as line items.
  • Debris type. Household mixed waste, construction debris, and clean concrete are different tickets. Some materials need a dedicated box.
  • Placement. Street permits (city charge, not ours), after-hours delivery, and low-clearance sites.
  • Trip fees. Dry runs, blocked access, overfilled boxes they cannot legally haul, and extra relocations.

When you compare quotes, line up size, included tons, included days, and what happens if the load is heavy. The lowest headline price is often the one with the smallest weight cap.
