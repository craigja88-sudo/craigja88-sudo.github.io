Permits

Driveway vs street, then call the city

Permit rules are local. This page is the pattern that holds in most US cities — not a fee schedule. We will not invent what Houston, Miami-Dade, or anyone else charges.

On your driveway

If the dumpster sits entirely on private property (driveway, interior lot), many cities do not require a right-of-way permit. You still need clear truck access, a surface that can take the weight, and HOA or landlord approval if those apply.

In the street

A box in the public street, on a sidewalk, or blocking a lane is usually a city (sometimes county) permit. Expect cones, time limits, and a separate bill from the city — paid by you or passed through by the hauler. Ask before delivery day.

Who to ask

  • Your city’s public works, transportation, or sanitation desk — search “[your city] dumpster permit” or “right-of-way dumpster”.
  • Unincorporated areas: often the county, not the nearest big city.
  • HOA, condo association, or landlord: they can say no even when the city says yes.
  • The hauler: many will pull a street permit as a line item if you ask up front.

We do not list municipal fees here. They change, they differ by duration and street type, and copying a blog’s dollar amount is how people get a ticket. If a quote includes “permit,” ask whether that is the city’s actual charge or the hauler’s service fee to file it.

Placement that avoids a permit fight

  • Keep the box off sidewalks, fire hydrants, and disabled parking.
  • Do not block a neighbor’s driveway or a bike lane.
  • Protect the driveway with boards if the hauler agrees — the truck is heavier than the dumpster.
  • In coastal and hurricane-prone cities, extra rules can appear around storm warnings. Confirm if weather is in the forecast.

City pages note the local pattern (heat, storms, HOAs) and still send you to the city to confirm.
